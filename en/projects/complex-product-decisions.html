Order on the page

When I started serving the Infra team in 2020, I didn't even know what a "log" was. Looking back, the complexity of engineering-infrastructure products has two sources: the huge technical system behind the pages, and the people who use it to work. Design can't eliminate complexity — it can only translate the system's complexity into order on the page; and to do that, you first have to load the whole system into your head.

01 / From nouns to a blueprint

First, load the whole system into your head

In 2020 I became Infra's founding designer, with no technical background. Logs, services, containers, storage, middleware — these words that technical people take for granted were all foreign concepts to me, each requiring its own way to understand.

My learning channels were more than one, and each needed its own calibration: searching online was scattered and unsystematic; asking technical colleagues meant judging the accuracy of what they said — everyone carries their own understanding, not necessarily objective; pure technical language I couldn't follow, so I relied on analogy, then confirmed and verified again and again. Understanding is built up, from micro to macro, with no skippable layer: nouns are atoms, products are molecules, the relationships between products are their bonds, and the whole platform blueprint is the assembled whole — and every layer has to answer the same question: who's using it, and what do they need to accomplish.

Member designers don't actually need to load the whole system into their heads the way I do. I have to, because I'm responsible for the team: managing, developing, and quality-checking internally, communicating with technical leads externally, and filling in wherever there's a gap — this position requires both breadth and depth. And all that understanding lands, finally, on concrete pages.

03 / Organizing complexity, not eliminating it

Organizing complexity, not eliminating it

Once it's clear, the design work is to organize complexity, not eliminate it: filters must keep explaining the operation's scope, related information must be read together, the PageHeader shrinks on scroll to give the space back to content — every decision helps users keep their bearings inside a complex system.

Understanding also has to be handoff-able. I started mapping the whole landscape before products took shape, turning products, users, and relationships into a shareable outline; later, three quarterly reports and two hundred-plus pages of material record exactly this process — from the 2020 product map to the 2021 thirteen-product-line landscape. This let the team share one map instead of each carrying their own imagination to work. The original report material can be shown sanitized in a face-to-face conversation.

When I did the design system, I found it was the same thing: the v4→v5 upgrade material was, at root, teaching stakeholders to understand the design system's overall mechanism. Complex business systems and design systems are the same problem in different domains: first load the system into your own head, then load it into more people's heads. This upgrade material can also be shown sanitized in a face-to-face conversation.

This understanding finally has to pass to the next collaborator: which information to read together, why configuration and results are separated. Understand the system first, and the page knows how to be drawn; write the understanding down, and the person taking over doesn't have to understand it from zero again.
