01 / The lifespan of the middle layer

Two hot products, after they quieted down

Reading the announcement of CozeByteDance's agent and workflow platform for orchestrating node flows.'s enterprise-plan changes reminded me of two products I'd worked on. SmartAn AI app-building platform for agents and workflows: agent configuration, workflow orchestration, testing, and publishing. Ask's executable SOPs are built with its workflow mode. Covered in the Ask project.'s workflow mode is similar to Coze's — not because either copied the other; internally, companies tend to build their own for information security. AskAn AI product for first-line on-call: the user reports an issue, AI analyzes and acts, and on-call experts authorize, correct, or take over at key points.'s executable SOPs use the same mode. When we built these, they were both company-level key projects, running hot; now both have quieted down.

The lifespan of a middle layer that fills a model's gap equals the lifespan of the gap. Back then models couldn't plan reliably on their own, and workflows gave them a structure to lean on; once models can plan, the premise of that layer disappears. Looking back, the problem wasn't whether the design was right then — it's whether we kept asking: does the user still need this form?

02 / Products expire

Products along the way have value — and expire

This isn't failure; it's process. The industry treats AGI as the main line, and everything else as forms along the road. Liang Wenfeng said it more bluntly in a May 2026 closed-door conversation with investors: C-side and B-side are both byproducts on the way to AGI — both intermediate outputs. Workflows and SOP platforms are exactly such forms: they let things run at a certain stage, and they'll step aside once the gap is filled.

Design systems don't belong in that category. They don't fill a model's gap — they turn judgment into a foundation asset. Whoever does the design needs the same constraints; after AI joins design, it's simply another user of the asset. Product forms expire; assets change users.

Having built these products wasn't wasted: they left a dividing line — what is execution, what is judgment. The forms will step aside; the line won't.

The specific designs of Smart and Ask are in the Resolving a user's report —AI and experts each doing their part. project; here I'm placing them in the process: necessary forms within a wave, not the destination.

03 / What gets absorbed is execution

What gets absorbed is execution; what can't be taken over is judgment

What exactly is this dividing line? What models absorb is the work of translating defined judgment into output: breaking down flows, drawing nodes, writing component specs. What can't be taken over is four things:

Defining the problem
what's worth solving, and to what degree it counts as solved.
Owning the consequence
when a judgment is wrong, who pays the cost and how it's settled.
Ambiguous trade-offs
when two options both make sense, which one and why.
Persuading and handing over
making the doer willing, and the receiver able to carry it.

AskAn AI product for first-line on-call: the user reports an issue, AI analyzes and acts, and on-call experts authorize, correct, or take over at key points.'s experience is these four things productized: let AI take on tasks while humans authorize, correct, and take over at key points — putting "humans hold the judgment" into the product structure. The answer to adapting to AI isn't learning tools; it's redesigning the division of labor.

04 / Where the time goes

Putting the choice back into my own work

In July 2026, the National Business Daily verified and published the transcript of a three-hour-forty-four-minute closed-door meeting between Liang Wenfeng and investors, held that May. What drew me wasn't the posture, but how he ordered his goals: last year, not pushing the C-side was right — a bigger prize is still ahead; the earlier opportunities were just small wins. C-side and B-side should be done, but they're byproducts, not the goal. Every investment should have that kind of reasoning.

The same question applies to the SkillA reusable instruction package for AI: organizing specs, knowledge, and process so AI works the team's way. I made this year: I can't accept that models change workflows, while also requiring the rules I wrote to stay forever. What I can do is keep trying, and keep checking the result: does the new method make it easier for people to finish the task? Did the saved steps push the trouble onto someone else?

Outside work, I've made small things with AI: a national-park roam, a classical-poem experience. They're still immature, but they let me go from deciding what to make all the way to handing it to someone else to experience. I don't require myself to first find something grand enough — I require that every time I start, I know who it helps and what it solves; and after finishing, I can admit how far it still is from the original intention.
