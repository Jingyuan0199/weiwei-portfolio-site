How do you spot complex-product design ability in hiring?

I've done 150 campus and experienced-hire interviews, and mentored every new member of the Infra design team. Mentoring and interviewing confirmed one thing: what's worth asking isn't what they've done, but how they work.

01 / Look at behavior, not the résumé label

Let new performance revise the first impression

In one interview, I recognized the candidate's handling of detail-level experience, but still had doubts about his complex-domain ability — so I kept it for the written test to verify. He took the initiative to learn the professional background, proposed multiple approaches to one problem and compared their trade-offs, and even questioned whether an existing feature was necessary, then verified its usage through a quick interview. The ability I hadn't seen clearly before now had clearer evidence, in those concrete behaviors.

There's the opposite case too: the conversation was initially satisfying, but he didn't pass the written test. He described the process fluently, but the understanding of the technical background and the reasoning into a solution weren't deep enough. That worries me more than a plain "not familiar": with the former I have to spend time figuring out how much he actually knows; with the latter I know exactly what to verify next.

My judgment in hiring formed gradually: as a dotted-line lead, hiring wasn't mine — whoever got hired, I used, and I could only arrange work around each person's traits; later, after the team merged into Infra, I ran the professional interview rounds as an expert and truly took part in the selection. That experience taught me that the standard for judging people has to be re-calibrated, again and again, in mentoring and in use.

02 / Initiative and honesty

In interviews, I look at two things

In interviews, I focus on two things:

Initiative · what you do with the problem
Just complete what's asked, or take the initiative to verify information beyond the question and propose a different approach. The former can execute; the latter can find their own way when the outcome is unclear.
Honesty · what you do with what you don't know
Saying directly "I don't have enough information yet; I'll verify and get back" is more credible than talking around it or forcing a conclusion — in one sentence you can see they know the gap, know where to find the answer, and dare to give a time.

My own evaluation follows the same rule: say clearly what I've seen and what I can't yet confirm. Different responsibility levels need different evidence: junior roles look at fundamentals and learning transfer; senior roles need to fully own a complex problem and drive it to delivery. Reliably completing your own project, versus coordinating others' work, handling disagreement, and providing ongoing support — those need different things to observe.

03 / Responsibility over surface performance

Judgment doesn't end at the offer

Real tasks after hire fill in what the interview couldn't show. In mentoring and daily collaboration, I focus on four things:

First reaction after a mistake
Fill the gap, or blame the outside? One slip is coincidence; whether the same thing recurs shows whether the lesson was absorbed.
Initiative when only the result is given
Can they break out the path themselves and align at key points? For the first kind I review their proposal; for the second, I'm effectively doing the proposal for them.
Honesty facing the unknown
Dare they say "I don't know" and give a deadline to verify? Someone who fakes understanding makes me spend twice the time telling what's real from what's not.
Boundaries when taking a task
Can they say "I can go this far, and I need support beyond this"? Someone who can't state their boundary hides the risk in their own hands until it collapses.

One member liked to dig in independently, and wouldn't sync up in time when stuck — often asking for help very late. It wasn't irresponsibility, but when the process information stayed in his own hands, the risk pressed only on him. Later I gave him a fixed product line and agreed to sync proactively at key points — independent judgment should stay, but surfacing risk in time also has to become a habit.

The four things look scattered, but they point at one thing: is this person willing to take responsibility for the result? Skills and completeness, mentoring can fill; willingness to take responsibility, it almost can't. I've also met agreeable, positive members whose collaboration felt good but whose delivery missed the mark — agreeableness isn't capability, and a good attitude isn't delivery. A strength someone talks up well still has to be proven by real work behavior.

An interview conclusion shouldn't become a fixed label stuck on a person. Introversion, accent, first-interview nerves — none of these can replace the answer to one question: can they clarify information, respond to disagreement, and move collaboration forward. Some people not chosen for the current role might fit better in another kind of product or team — an evaluation is only useful to both sides when it can name the specific gap, and also name the support the team couldn't offer at the time.
