01 / Qualitative to find problems, quantitative to see scale

User research: replacing "going by feel" with "having evidence"

The thing design fears most is deciding by feel: something feels wrong, so you change it directly. User research does the opposite — before changing anything, first understand who the user is, what the task is, and where they get stuck. Our basic approach is qualitative first to find problems, then quantitative to see scale — two methods, each with its own job:

Qualitative research · interviews and observation
Small sample, answers "why" — have users replay a real task, observe the pauses, detours, and moments they ask for help, listen to them think out loud. Used to find problems and form hypotheses, not for statistical generalization.
Quantitative research · questionnaires and data
Larger sample, answers "how many" — how many people a problem affects, how to rank priorities. It can't explain causes, so it's used to verify scale.

Small-scope improvements need only one round of qualitative work; when the blast radius is large and you need to rank priorities, add quantitative verification.

The Infra Design SystemInfra Design System (InfraD), built and maintained by design and front-end teams; connects components, business patterns, page templates, usage guides, and Design Tokens, supporting multi-product theming and continuous evolution. website is a complete example. It had been in use for years, through several version iterations; in 2025 we ran a round of systematic user research to understand how front-end developers, TPMTechnical Product Manager: owns technical products' requirements, progress, and cross-team coordination.s, and designers actually use these assets — where they start, where they get stuck. The starting point was a common piece of feedback: "I don't use the website much." But asking further, the reasons differ: some lack content, some can't find the entry, some finished the task elsewhere long ago. It shouldn't just become the same "redesign the website" request.

The qualitative interviews covered 12 participants: 5 front-end developers, 4 designers, 3 TPMs. We didn't ask them to evaluate the site in general terms; we asked each to recall a recent real request and replay the full "find — choose — configure" path on the site and the new demo, recording pauses, confusion, and Think-aloudAn observation method: ask users to speak their thoughts as they operate, capturing real reasoning rather than after-the-fact recall. moments, and noting when they turned to other tools.

Those moments carry more signal than scores. One front-end developer said that when a component's information was incomplete, he'd jump straight to Ant DesignA widely used React component system; InfraD's base components are built on it, keeping the interactions while adjusting color and radius.; another said the document versions in FigmaThe design and collaboration tool: component libraries, prototypes, and delivery. were messy and he didn't know which component library was the official latest — neither was "using the site badly"; one lacked content, the other lacked the certainty of "which one is right." We also asked a sharper question: have you ever mistaken a feature for nonexistent because you couldn't find it? The cost of "can't find it" is never just a few wasted minutes — it can discount a person's trust in the whole system.

On the quantitative side, role-specific questionnaires added coverage. The output was 15 categories of recommendations, split into website and Figma tasks, each with its priority and engineering dependency recorded. Every recommendation had to state its evidence: the same "components are hard to find" could be a category-naming problem, or not knowing which category to pick — the former means adjusting the entry, the latter means adding a selection guide. After finding a piece of feedback, you can't jump straight to a feature.

02 / Three tools, one standard

Experience measurement: making quality measurable

Beyond research, the team co-built a set of experience measurement made of three tools, each solving a different problem:

Measurement model · set the standard
Break "is the experience good or bad" into three dimensions — effectiveness (can you finish the task, achieve the goal), ease of use (how easy to learn and use), and acceptance (willing to keep using it). Drawing on Two international standardsISO 9241-11 (classic usability: effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction) and ISO/IEC 25022 (quality-in-use framework), used to calibrate the experience-measurement dimensions.' classic definitions of usability, combined with Infra's product experience dimensions (performance, achievement, emotion) and design values (efficient, natural, flexible, open, intelligent co-existence), it lands on concrete metrics.
Design self-check list · designer self-review
Before entering review, check against the list: the thinking layer looks at product goals, user needs, and industry context; the construction layer looks at information architecture, functional scenarios, and primary/secondary tasks; rules that don't apply may be removed. Only after passing self-check does it go to review.
Quality evaluation rules · reviewer scoring
Each rule is scored on a five-level agreement scale (from strongly disagree to strongly agree), with sub-items under effectiveness and ease of use carrying weights. It rates "passing" not "pretty" — it raises the floor, not the ceiling; defects in critical flows can't be offset by an average score.

Why this set is needed: before, whether a design was good or bad was "by feel" — problems couldn't name their location, discussions couldn't land on the same standard, and improvement couldn't be measured. Once experience becomes measurable data, collaboration gains a shared language. These three tools were co-built by the team; I took part.

03 / Facts, proposals, and open questions

Design Prephase: letting materials flow into the next round

Requirements live in chat, business rules in documents, reference products in another form — what early-stage design fears most is scattered materials and inconsistent understanding. The Design PrephaseA team co-built early-stage practice: sort requirements and sources, distinguish confirmed facts, proposals, and open questions, and form the next round's design input. we built first sorts requirements into three kinds: confirmed facts, proposed approaches, and unanswered questions, then organizes the confirmed objects, flows, and states into the input for the next round of design. Later it was packaged as a plugin — the plugin is only the form; the content is the point. It has seven capabilities:

Requirements Organizer
Extract design information from meetings, chat, and transcripts; separate confirmed rules from open questions; keep the original semantics.
Feature Benchmarking
First form a sourced material pack, then compare reference products task by task; compare specific tasks and design choices, not copy interfaces.
Design Definition
Explain scope, structure, states, and user operation flows from existing materials; avoid forcing parallel features into a linear flow.
AI Design Input
Centrally maintain confirmed logic, current page context, and adopted references, to reduce contradictions across downstream versions.
SeaTalk Feedback
Analyze questions and replies in the team's internal communication tool (SeaTalkThe team's internal messaging tool; requirements and feedback are often discussed there.); distinguish capability gaps, failures, discoverability, permissions, and experience; trace conclusions back to the original feedback.
Skill Audit
Record output quality, user feedback, and revision issues, as evidence for adjusting the workflow later.
Workflow Routing
Check existing outputs and continue from the current stage; enter the next step as actually needed, avoiding re-sorting.

The three methods point at the same thing: making judgment evidence-based. User research tells me what to change next, measurement gives quality a standard, and the early-stage method lets materials continue. For an improvement, what to watch afterward isn't traffic either: whether users found it, used it right, and kept using it — that's the real change.
