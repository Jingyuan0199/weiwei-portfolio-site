Infra Design System (InfraD) is an enterprise design system that connects components, business patterns, page templates, and usage guidance. A systematic Design Tokens layer and product theming let different products share a foundation while keeping the expressions they need.

I also wrote the full component design guides, kept supporting usage, and fed business needs and feedback back into the system.

01 / From scattered tools to a shared foundation

Scattered tools, one shared foundation

When I entered Infra in 2020, I found engineering teams building their own internal tools. The same table, filter, and action were written differently in different products. Every new request risked re-solving a problem that had already been solved.

I led Infra Design System from zero: components, assets, and standards filled in step by step, with implementation co-maintained by the front-end team. As more products converged into Space, their differences landed in the same place.

Even after components unified, business pages still had heavy repetition. A typical management page has a page title and global actions, a filter area, a table, and batch actions. I kept turning these reusable combinations into business assets and page templates, so the next design could start from an existing structure.

Templates give a page a starting point; Patterns record how search, edit, and feedback scenarios are handled; the object relationships, permissions, and trade-offs in a specific business still come back to the task itself.

02 / Understand the rules first, then translate them into assets

An upgrade that started with understanding the rules

As business assets piled up, the underlying styles got harder to maintain together. Change the primary color and the page could still be left with uneven blues; a dark-mode attempt ran into unclear hierarchy, janky switching, and flicker. Just swapping colors in the mockup didn't fix any of it. When I drove the v4 → v5 design-side upgrade in 2024, I spent real effort understanding the Design Tokens mechanism — without a technical background, how the values were derived wasn't obvious at first: why does changing one base value affect so many places? What should a page's title, description, and disabled text each reference?

I first untangled Seed / Map / Alias. Seed supplies the starting points — primary color, base type size, unit sizes. Algorithms generate Maps from those — color ramps, type scales, spacing. Alias then maps those values to actual uses. That way, when designing, you first judge what role a piece of text plays, then pick the right reference.

Once I understood the mechanism, I turned it into a maintainable variable structure on the design side and cleaned up the bindings and references in components. Page templates carried the same semantics: titles, labels, descriptions, and placeholders each had a rationale, so business designers no longer had to pick a 'similar-looking' color each time.

It also changed how I discuss styles with the front-end team. When we modify or customize, we confirm purpose and value along the same token name, then apply it to the design assets and the code separately.

03 / After the upgrade, what concretely changed

What concretely changed in Space after the upgrade

Clear value rationale at design time

Take the MetaInfo at the top of a page: title color, description color, label color, and action-group padding all map to named values. Designers judge a content's role and hierarchy first, then pick a rule — no more hunting through similar values from memory.

Spacing got a shared scale. Title-to-body, filter-to-table, adjacent actions — you still choose distances by relationship, but you pick from one scale. Pages accumulate with readable hierarchy, and are easier to review and maintain.

Change one rule, skip the search

Adding a text-color tier used to mean finding and replacing everywhere — design plus engineering, a 2–3 week round trip. After the upgrade, it's a centralized token update, sometimes half an hour.

The real change is how you change: once pages and components reference tokens, an update propagates along shared rules. More places to update no longer means treating each one as a separate problem. The full upgrade is in the v4 → v5 upgrade notes.

The front-end follows the same references

In code, colors, spacing, and radii went from scattered literals to token references. When design and front-end check a value, we can say why it's that value and what changing it affects. For special business needs, it's also easier to judge whether to follow the global rule or keep a local component config.

04 / A shared foundation, with room for each product

A shared foundation, with room for each product

Over time the UI Kit I maintain accumulated themes — Default / Space, Smart, Ask, AIOps. Different products have different moods and expression needs; theming lets them keep evolving on the same foundation.

In the actual configuration, shared variables and component exceptions coexist. Smart and Ask both use radius 8 for the general scale, but the Tag's local radius is 4 in Smart and 16 in Ask. Button references the global config; Tag keeps a local setting that fits the product's expression.

In Smart I gave theming guidance and supported business-component co-building; designing Ask, I kept working on its business components on the shared foundation. Entering DataSuite, I had to respect existing implementations, technical differences, and maintenance resources — adopting gradually, and never completing full unification.

These experiences left me with one standing question when maintaining the system: which differences are worth maintaining together, and which should each product decide for itself? The answer keeps correcting itself with the business.

05 / Getting assets into daily work

Getting the assets into daily work

With Space spanning a hundred-plus products, the design team can't redraw every routine page from scratch. Once components and templates were in place, some routine requests could be assembled by TPMs on top of templates, with designers stepping in for the key judgments and quality checks.

But having the assets isn't the same as using them well. I wrote the full component Design Guides and kept collecting Patterns, published on the docs site. Component guides explain how to choose a type, arrange primary and secondary, and meet interaction and layout requirements; Patterns give reference from real scenarios; Templates give an editable starting point.

Day to day, I also helped colleagues through training, reviews, walkthroughs, and guidance on specific requests. When usage got hard or new needs kept appearing, I decided whether to add a guide, adjust a component, or leave it as the business's own pattern. The front-end co-maintains implementation; I keep maintaining the design assets and usage rationale — and the foundation keeps updating through repeated use.

06 / Next: handing what I've built to AI

Next: handing these assets to AI

This year I started letting AI use these assets: operating Figma through MCP, constraining Figma Make with Design MD, and having Codex generate interfaces from existing components in a React project. Each path has limits — standard screens are relatively easy; requests that need creative judgment are still hard.

Specs give generation a basis, but they don't guarantee the task and the approach have been thought through. I'll keep turning experience into content AI can use — and keep testing it in real tasks. The specific problems and design debt in this exploration are in the essay 'Distilling experience into AI.'
